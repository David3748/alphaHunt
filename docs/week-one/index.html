The hunt for uncorrelated alpha, week one

350,456 SEC filings · 10,881 scored events · one week · everything below regenerates from the repo

My bio says I'm looking for uncorrelated alpha, so I decided to stop saying it and go find some. The question: can a large language model read SEC filings well enough to rank stock returns out of sample? This is the story in the order it happened — the idea, the machine, the graveyard of failed signals, the one result that survived every way I tried to kill it, two companies worth knowing about, and the prediction board sitting at the end of the pipeline right now.

Chapter 1 · The idea
Every "GPT picks stocks" demo is broken by construction

An LLM asked about a company's 2015 annual report has almost certainly seen what happened in 2016. It knows which names went bankrupt, which got acquired, which ten-baggerged. Ask it to analyze the filing and you're not testing reading comprehension — you're testing recall wearing foresight's clothes.

There are four ways to cheat without meaning to:

Input lookahead — evidence contains documents filed after decision time. Parametric leakage — training data already holds the outcome. Survivorship — the universe is today's listings; the failures that would have punished the strategy were quietly deleted. Selection — try twenty rules, publish the winner.

So before running a single model call, I built the thing that makes cheating checkable. Filings carry their exact SEC acceptance timestamps in a bitemporal store. Every prediction references an immutable manifest that rejects any document the market couldn't have seen yet. Blobs are SHA-256-addressed, so nothing mutates silently. Protocols get written to disk before outcomes exist; amendments are separate frozen documents, and whatever period they peeked at gets excluded from their confirmatory claims. Significance testing shuffles scores within calendar months, which preserves regime timing while destroying ranking information, then reruns the whole portfolio simulation 500 times.

Chapter 2 · The machine
Eight stages from raw SEC bytes to a graded prediction

The failures and the wins come later. First the machine itself, because the pipeline is the result: everything below executes as eight resumable stages, and every stage caches its output per-item. Interrupt a run and it picks up where it stopped, recomputing nothing, re-billing nothing. Here is the whole machine:

Stages 1–4: building a universe that couldn't have been cherry-picked

① Enumerate. The SEC publishes structured XBRL datasets quarterly. Every 10-K, 10-Q, 20-F, and 40-F from 2009–2025 lands here: 350,456 filings from 15,387 issuers, each carrying its acceptance timestamp to the second. No vendor lists, no "companies that exist today" — the population is whatever the regulator received.

② Resolve symbols. Filings don't contain tickers by default, so each filing's own XBRL instance is parsed for its dei:TradingSymbol fact — the ticker that filing claimed at that moment, which is how delisted-and-renamed companies stay reachable. 25,343 issuer/stem regimes resolved; anything unresolvable is discarded rather than guessed.

③ Preprice. Every symbol gets a 370-day daily price history ending at each filing's date. Then the eligibility gates bite: US-listed exchange, ≥120 prior closes, price ≥ $1, 30-day average dollar volume ≥ $1M, drawdown ≤ −40% from the one-year high, and one signal per issuer per 180 days. Note what's not a gate: any future outcome. Companies that later went bankrupt remain fully eligible — their filings just also tend to lack liquid histories, which the funnel shows honestly.

④ Build cases. Each eligible filing becomes an evidence pack: the primary document's text (up to ~220k characters), plus exact acceptance metadata, frozen into a content-addressed case. The model will never see anything but this snapshot.

Stages 5–6: the model layer — five specialists and a skeptic-by-construction synthesis

⑤ Extract. Each case is read five times, once per lens, each seeing a different 60,000-character window chosen by keyword targeting (the liquidity lens sees cash-runway language; governance sees director-dealing and listing-risk language). Every claim must carry an exact verbatim quote, verified programmatically against the source text. Ungrounded claims survive in the audit trail but are stripped before any downstream use. Grounding rates ran 65–75% depending on lens.

⑥ Synthesize. Two independent synthesis calls consume only the structured lens outputs — never raw text — and must emit calibrated fields: probability of beating SPY by 20%+ over 90 days, probability of any positive excess, expected excess, downside-tail probability, a decision label, and thesis/catalyst/invalidation narratives. Independence matters: replicate disagreement gets measured instead of averaged away, which is how I know the score spread isn't noise-free theater.

Liquiditycash runway, debt maturities, covenants, dilution capacity · grounding 70%
Operationsmargins, backlog, demand, unit economics · grounding 75%
Catalystsfunded, dated events within 90–180 days vs aspirations · grounding 68%
Accountingearnings quality, restatements, auditor signals, recognition risk · grounding ~72%

Stages 7–8: grading under oath

⑦ Evaluate. Entry is the first tradable close after the SEC acceptance timestamp (same-day only if accepted before market close); exit is 90 calendar days later; costs are 25bp per side; idle cash earns the 13-week T-bill yield; the benchmark is exposure-matched SPY (tuned to what the book actually holds, so style bets earn no phantom credit). Selection uses expanding top-decile thresholds computed only from strictly-earlier scores. Significance comes from month-blocked placebos: 500 shuffles that preserve exactly when signals could fire while destroying what the scores said.

⑧ Archive. Every artifact compresses into a checksummed manifest and syncs to cold storage: 4 GB of raw runs becomes 804 MB of zstd archives. Any number on this page can be regenerated from those archives byte-for-byte.

That's the machine. What I did with it first was mostly embarrassing.

Chapter 4 · The real test
Seventeen years, and the original strategy fails

2009–2025: 350,456 structured filings filtered to 9,885 eligible events, each read by five specialist lenses (liquidity, operations, catalysts, accounting, governance) and synthesized twice, roughly 69,000 calls running unattended on a VM while I slept. The originally deployed score ran first against gates frozen weeks earlier.

It failed. Sharpe 0.74 versus a required 1.0. Max drawdown −31.8% versus −25%. Publishing that feels bad. It's also the point: the gates existed precisely so that moment would be a non-event instead of a temptation.

Three replacement rules had been pre-registered before any outcome opened, tested only on holdout cohorts with discovery years excluded and Bonferroni correction across exactly three hypotheses:

The row that matters is the mechanical baseline losing. "Buy whatever is crushed" earned −0.09 IR over seventeen years while the model's probability fields earned about +1.5. (IR is information ratio: excess return per unit of risk, exposures matched. Half a point is respectable; 1.5 made me re-check my own code.) Whatever the signal is, it isn't repackaged dip-buying.

Chapter 5 · The memory problem
But does it remember the answers?

Here's the uncomfortable part: both holdout decades sit inside any plausible training window. Confirming on 2018 data doesn't exclude a model that memorized 2018. So I ran a blind experiment — 110 cases re-scored with every identifier scrubbed from the text: names, tickers, commission file numbers, even IRS employer IDs replaced with placeholders. Same prompts, same model. If the signal lives in memory, blinding kills it.

Performance barely moves; blind-vs-identified scores agree at ρ = .836. With no idea who the company is, the model reaches nearly the same conclusions from the prose alone. Verdict under the frozen decision rule: genuine. Caveats stand — addresses weren't scrubbed, n=110 is modest, and nothing rules out paraphrase-level leakage. The unbounded control is the live cohort at the end of this story.

Chapter 6 · Case files
Two companies tell you what this actually looks like

Aggregates hide texture. Here are three names, chosen because each teaches something different.

Case file № 1 — DT: the one the model got right

Signal date February 9, 2026. Digital Ocean, down 46% from its high, scored 26.5 on p(+20%) against a 21.5 threshold: top-decile conviction with a long_candidate decision. Entered the next session, held ninety days: +11.2% stock vs +6.8% SPY, +4.4% excess. The only graded winner in the lead rule's book. This is what a hit looks like: modest, fast, unremarkable. Most winning trades look like this; the fantasy version looks like the next case.

Case file № 2 — WIX: the one that got away, badly

March 5, 2026. Wix, −58% from its high, score 27.0, model decision long_candidate. Third-highest conviction name in the book. Ninety days later: −42.8% stock vs +12.9% SPY, for −55.7 points of excess. This is what it means when people say single-name risk dwarfs ranking skill: the best-laid read on a quality franchise, timed one leg of a falling knife too early. Seven graded trades, one winner, mean −35%. The rule will live or die on the accumulating sample, not on either anecdote. But anecdotes are why position sizing exists.

Case file № 3 — PRCT: the document nobody reads

A parallel crawler mined obscure public sources — GDELT, the Federal Register, ClinicalTrials.gov, openFDA — over issuers from the scored universe. Of 11,873 documents analyzed, an analyst→skeptic→consensus gate rejected 88%, kept 1,438 watches, and passed exactly one long candidate: PROCEPT BioRobotics, flagged via a November 2019 Federal Register rule (1.9 million characters of routine Medicare payment policy. The consensus record refused to trust the excerpt and demanded verification, so I pulled the full text:

"After consideration of the public comments we received, we believe the AquaBeam System meets the cost criterion and we are approving it for device pass-through payment status beginning in CY 2020." CY 2020 OPPS/ASC final rule, FR 2019-24138 — verified word-for-word, August 2026

Buried reimbursement approval preceding an adoption inflection: the exact pattern the miner hunts, recovered end-to-end. Not tradable as history, since the document predates PRCT's IPO. But look where the stock went after listing, and where it stands now: down 78% from peak, HYDROS ramping, a prostate-cancer readout pending:

What the machine believes right now

Which brings us to the present tense. These are the standing signals from the June 30 data cutoff — ranked by the confirmed p(+20%) field, thresholds calibrated causally on everything strictly earlier, no outcome peeked at. Some windows have partially elapsed already; none have been re-graded since the table above, because the grading script doesn't take suggestions.

All three rules converge on RCKT, VRDN, WIX, and DT — rule agreement rather than rule noise. The model's own decision field flags WIX, DT, IMRX, ZG, INTU, GWRE as long_candidate. If you're keeping score at home: the last time it used that label this confidently, seven of them lost money and one made four points. That's the honest state of the art.

Three things survive contact with the whole week. The infrastructure is the alpha — every number above means something only because acceptance timestamps, manifests, frozen gates, and placebos made cheating checkable. Most signals are fake, and you cannot eyeball which — four of six families died on contact with out-of-sample data. And live data does not care about your backtest: the first forward print is negative, it's published anyway, and the remaining windows mature through November. The hunt continues.
