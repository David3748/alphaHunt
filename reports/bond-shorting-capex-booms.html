Shorting Bonds During Major Capex Booms

Seven historical episodes — US railroads, the 1920s, Japan's bubble, telecom fiber, China's build-out, US shale, and today's AI data centers — examined for one question: when capex euphoria peaks, does shorting bonds actually pay? And what kind of bonds?

01The Thesis

Capital-expenditure booms are among the most reliable generators of credit destruction in financial history. Booms are typically financed at the margin by debt — railroad mortgage bonds in the 1850s, holding-company bonds in the 1920s, convertible and warrant bonds in Japan, junk and high-yield in telecom, LGFV chengtou bonds in China, and HY energy bonds in shale. When the build-out outruns demand, bondholders are the residual losers: defaulted debt is wiped out while equity holders at least get the call option on a turnaround.

Yet the historical record is sharply two-sided. The most obvious short — against government duration — usually fails during the boom itself, because monetary easing and flight-to-safety dominate at the moment of peak stress. The trade that works is shorting credit risk in the sectors doing the capex, or shorting the sovereign only in a narrow window around the terminal rate hike — a window that, in Japan's case, opened roughly nine months after equities peaked.

Every boom produces a bond trade that works. The discipline is to pick the instrument — duration, spread, or sector credit — whose loss function matches the boom's structure, and to remember that the biggest bond gains arrive after the bust begins, not before.

02The Framework: Three Kinds of "Bond Short"

1 · Duration / Rates
Shorting the risk-free curve
Betting yields rise (prices fall) on government bonds. Only pays while the boom generates overheating that forces terminal hikes — after which central banks cut hard and the short gets annihilated (Japan: 8% → <1%).
2 · Credit Spreads
Shorting credit, not rates
Betting sector or index spreads widen as leverage-fuelled capex overbuilds. This is where the money actually was: telecom 2000–02, energy 2014–16, AI data-center HY 2025–26.
3 · Sector Credit
Shorting the boom's debt
Single-name or sub-sector shorts on the issuers doing the capex. Highest alpha, highest risk: requires the boom to end in defaults (WorldCom, Global Crossing, Winstar, E&P filers, LGFVs).

The telecom episode (1996–2002) is the canonical illustration of why #1 fails while #2/#3 pay: from December 2000 to September 2002 the Fed cut from 6.5% to 1.25% and the 10-yr Treasury yield fell from 5.12% to 3.87% — a big loss for a duration short — while the high-yield spread widened to a record 10.24%, telecom spreads to ~21%, and WorldCom's bonds traded at 11–20 cents on the dollar.

03Railroads 1837–1879

The First Leveraged Infrastructure Boom

Duration short: mixed Credit short: strong

US railroad mileage exploded from 2,818 mi (1840) to 9,021 (1850), 30,626 (1860) and 93,267 (1880). The 1850s alone added 21,605 miles; the 1870s added ~40,000. Financing shifted decisively from equity to debt — railroad mortgage bonds at ~7% coupons, 20–30 year maturities, sold heavily to British and German capital. By 1859 railroad securities outstanding exceeded $1.1bn, with ~$700m raised in a single decade; 1880–84 issuance alone hit $2.72bn (~4.8% of GNP).

The boom ended in the three great 19th-century panics — each a credit event for bondholders. In the 1837/42 state-default crisis, canal/state bonds were near-wiped-out as Pennsylvania, Maryland, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Mississippi and Louisiana defaulted (four repudiated). In 1857, high-grade rail yields jumped from 9.6% (Jan) to 11.9% (Oct) as Illinois Central, Reading and others defaulted. In 1873, Jay Cooke's $100m Northern Pacific 7.3% gold-bond underwriting collapsed with the firm; 89 railroads failed, and by 1875 half of US rail mileage was in receivership. Giesecke et al. estimate 36% of the corporate bond market by par value defaulted within three years.

Verdict: The great money was shorting default-risk credit — state/canal bonds 1839–42, speculative construction bonds 1857 and 1873 — where losses ran 80–100%. But the aggregate high-grade index only spiked at panic moments (1857: +2.3pp; 1873: +0.6pp, quickly reversed), and yields fell through the 1870s deflationary bust. A broad duration short was marginal; a targeted credit short was superb.

04The 1920s US Boom

An Equity Boom With Calm Bonds — Then a Credit Collapse

Duration short: lost Credit short: early = lost

The 1920s were a genuine capex boom — but an equity-financed one. Electric output rose 228% (1920–30); utility plant book values grew from ~$3bn (1917) to ~$9.4bn (1927); autos trebled 1919–29; building investment hit ~12% of gross national income at the 1927 peak; rail/power/telecom construction totalled ~$20.4bn (1923–29).

Bonds, however, did not sell off. Long-term Treasury yields fell from ~5.7% (mid-1920) to ~3.2% (end-1927), rising only ~50bp to ~3.7% (mid-1929) on Fed tightening, then rallying again. Aaa corporates fell from 6.1% (1920) to 4.5% (1928). Shorting bonds during the boom would have lost money for nearly all of it. The Baa-vs-Treasury spread did not widen until fall 1930 — after the equity crash — then blew out to >7.5% by May 1932.

The wreckage came in the 1930s: defaulted corporate bonds rose from ~$0.5bn (1929) to ~$3.9bn — ~15% of outstandings by 1936. Railroad defaults went from 0.5% (1931) to 27.9% (1940); industrials in default rose from 2% to 24% of outstandings (1931–34). Over a quarter of domestic bonds issued in the 1920s defaulted in the 1930s. Railroad bond prices fell 30–50% in the 12 months to October 1931. The trade existed — but only for shorts opened after 1929, and it was a credit/deflation trade, not a rates trade.

Verdict: Shorting Treasury duration across 1920–29 failed. Shorting credit only worked from late 1930 — roughly a year after the equity top and driven by default risk plus forced bank selling. Timing mattered more than direction.

05Japan 1987–1998

The "Short Too Late" Trap, In Pure Form

JGB short: won a narrow window, then catastrophic Credit short: failed

Japan's capital formation ran ~30% of GDP through the high-growth era and hit an all-time 38.7% of GDP in 1973 — the highest capex intensity ever recorded in a major economy. The late-1980s bubble was less industrial than financial: equity-linked finance (shares, convertible and warrant bonds) exploded from ¥4tn (1983) to ¥28tn (1989), and Japanese firms issued $435bn of Eurobonds-with-warrants and convertibles in 1986–90 at ultra-low coupons, recycling the proceeds into equities via tokkin funds.

The JGB market gave shorters one clean, narrow window. The 10-yr yield rose from 4.80% (Jan 1989) to 5.52% (Dec 1989) as the BOJ hiked from 2.5% to 6.0%, peaking at 8.03% in September 1990. A short opened at the December 1989 Nikkei peak (38,957) captured ~250bp — roughly 15–18% on a 10-yr. But the yield peak came nine months after the equity peak. Anyone who held the short into 1991 was annihilated as yields collapsed to 5.72% (1991), 3.40% (1993), 1.94% (1997) and 0.88% (Oct 1998) — an 800bp one-way collapse that dwarfs the 320bp rise that preceded it.

Credit shorting failed because of the same force that crushed duration shorts: the BOJ's easing dominated credit risk. Even as the "Japan premium" pushed Japanese banks to pay 100–125bp over LIBOR (Dec 1997) and NPLs reached ¥28–77tn, JGB yields still fell. Central-bank easing trumped credit risk in the pricing of the sovereign curve.

Verdict: A surgical JGB short between late-1989 and mid-1990 worked. Any longer exposure was a disaster. The asymmetry — a ~320bp boom-time rise versus an ~800bp bust-time collapse — is the cleanest warning in this entire study.

06Telecom / Fiber 1996–2002

The Textbook Credit Short

Treasury short: lost Telecom credit short: huge winner

The fiber boom was financed almost entirely by bonds. US telecoms spent ~$444bn on capex from 1996–2001 and carried ~$500bn of debt; ~39 million fiber miles were laid at ~$90bn build cost — over 97% of it idle. Telecom was 30–40% of all new high-yield issuance; in the 12 months to June 2000 it was ~70% of all US sub-investment-grade issuance. WorldCom sold an $11.9bn bond in May 2001 and $17bn across 2001–02.

Spreads compressed hard through the build phase, then broke. HY spreads began widening steadily from mid-2000 — before the Nasdaq peak in March 2000's aftermath and before capex topped in 2000–01. The defaults came in waves: Winstar (Apr 2001), Global Crossing (Jan 2002), Adelphia, then WorldCom — the largest bankruptcy in US history (July 21, 2002), $41bn of debt, bonds trading at 11–20 cents. Telecom was 55% of default dollars in 2001 and 52% in 2002; the index HY default rate hit a record 12.8% in 2002. HY yield spreads went from ~700–800bp (mid-2000) to 1,010bp (Sep 30, 2002) and ~2,100bp for telecom itself.

Meanwhile the Fed cut from 6.5% to 1.25% and the 10-yr Treasury yield fell from 5.12% to 3.87%. Shorting Treasuries lost; shorting telecom credit made a fortune — ~100 cents to 11–20 cents on the biggest names.

Verdict: The cleanest historical proof that the bond short in a capex boom is a credit-spread short, opened as credit deteriorated (mid-2000) even while risk-free duration rallied. The move took ~2 years to play out — patience and carry mattered.

07China's Build-Out 2003–2015

Where the Sovereign Short Failed and the Shadow-Credit Short Mattered

CGB short: only 2007 & 2013 windows LGFV / shadow credit: the real exposure

China's boom was the largest in history: gross fixed capital formation rose from ~35% to ~47% of GDP by 2010 (vs ~20% in developed economies); crude steel output went from 101Mt (1996) to 779Mt (2013) — ~48% of world output; the RMB 4 trillion stimulus of 2008–09 pushed state-financed investment +60% in 2009. But the boom was financed by bank loans and local-government vehicles, not the sovereign bond market — which is why the CGB short mostly failed.

The 10-yr CGB hit an all-time high of ~4.8% in September 2007 (shorting worked, +150–160bp) and spiked to 4.72% in the June 2013 money-market squeeze, but traded ~3.0–3.6% through the hottest credit years of 2009–14 because the PBoC administered record-low rates, a savings glut and capital controls suppressed the sovereign curve, and credit ran through banks and LGFVs.

The actual debt bomb was the chengtou (LGFV) market, which grew ~85%/yr from 2008–2014: outstanding went from RMB 281bn (2008) to RMB 4.95tn (2014); stimulus loans to LGFVs were ~RMB 2.3tn in 2009; trust assets hit RMB 8.7tn. Chengtou issuance yields rose from ~4.7% (2009) to ~6.9% (2014); the 2011 Yunnan Highway default and the June 2013 repo squeeze (7-day repo spiked to 11.62%) were the stress markers. Commodity-exporting sovereigns (Australia, Brazil — upgraded to IG in April 2008) rallied on the terms-of-trade windfall via spread compression and FX, not via higher yields.

Verdict: An import-driven boom transmitted bond pain to the shadow-banking credit complex and to commodity exporters' spreads, not to the administered sovereign curve. Where bond markets float freely, shorting the sovereign (2007, 2013) worked; where the state controls rates, it didn't.

08US Shale 2011–2020

The "Growth For Growth's Sake" Debt Machine

Energy HY credit short: big winner Timing: needed patience, then crash-driven

Energy rose from ~4% of the US high-yield index (2006) to ~15% (2014), the second-largest HY sector, issuing record debt at 3–5x Debt/EBITDAX. E&P capex grew 17% y/y in 2011; energy became ~1/3 of total US business capex by 2014. Critically, the sector never generated positive free cash flow 2010–2019 — Deloitte pegged cumulative sector FCF at roughly −$300bn. This is the structural signature of a capex boom: production growth paid for with borrowed money.

HY OAS troughed at 3.36% (June 2014). Energy HY spreads were ~400bp mid-2014, then blew out to ~1,000bp by early 2015 as WTI fell from ~$107 to ~$40. Defaults followed the oil crash, not the capex peak: 42 E&P bankruptcies with ~$17.9bn debt in 2015, 70 filings / $56.8bn in 2016; energy was 56% of HY defaults in H1 2016. Recoveries were brutal — 21 cents on the dollar for 2015 energy bankruptcies vs a 59% historical average; unsecured bonds recovered 20–40 cents. The 2020 COVID collapse (WTI at −$37.63 on April 20, 2020) brought 55 more filings / $53bn. Cumulative 2015–21: 274 E&P plus 330 OFS/midstream filings, involving $321bn of debt.

On timing: energy HY began underperforming in 2H 2013 (before the crash) as credit quality deteriorated at the margin — a modest early warning — but the outsized returns were crash-driven. A 2013–14 short while capex was peaking eventually worked but required ~a year of carry and patience. Equity holders lost everything; secured bank lenders took the smallest losses; bondholders 20–40 cents.

Verdict: A sector-credit short paid massively, but it was fundamentally a crash trade triggered by oil, not a clean "overcapacity" trade. The lesson is that in capex booms financed by debt, credit stress arrives when the cash flow does not — and shale's cash flow never arrived.

09AI Data Centers 2023–2026

The Live Test Case

Treasury duration short: working so far (30y > 5%) AI/HY credit short: mixed, building

The largest capex boom ever measured is underway. Hyperscaler capex roughly doubled annually: ~$226bn (2024) → ~$388–410bn (2025) → ~$725–785bn guided for 2026, with Goldman projecting $7.6tn cumulative 2026–31 and Moody's flagging $1.2tn of off-balance-sheet leases plus $460bn of direct debt. The financing is migrating aggressively into the bond market: data-center ABS outstanding ~$49bn and growing (rated issuance $9.25bn in 2025 vs $3.9bn in 2024), private-credit AI loans >$200bn (BIS), record investment-grade issuance ($721bn IG in Q1 2026 alone), landmark deals including Nvidia's $25bn (June 2026), Alphabet's 100-year bond, Meta's $27bn+ SPV (PIMCO-anchored), and CoreWeave junk at 9.25%–11.5%.

Unlike the telecom/shale booms, this one has already moved long-term rates: the 30-yr Treasury topped 5% in May 2026 (~5.14%, highest since 2007) and traded ~5.28% in late August — a winner so far for duration shorts, as term premia reprice on deficits and AI-driven growth expectations. IG spreads are at 20-year tights (~+89bp) and HY ~2.79% — credit is still cheap, which is precisely the late-cycle danger the history warns about. Stress is visible at the edges: Moody's July 2026 circular-economy warning, Oracle at Baa2 negative, CoreWeave at Ba3 with 2031 bonds ~11.5%, data-center ABS widening since June 2026, and a widening hyperscaler-to-IG spread gap.

BofA's Michael Hartnett named shorting hyperscaler bonds a top 2026 trade; Ninety One cut credit exposure to ~zero; Michael Burry warns GPU depreciation is understated ~5–6 years (~$176bn overstatement). The consensus: any AI credit stress shows up first in HY/private structures and data-center ABS — not the AAA hyperscaler curve.

Verdict (so far): The duration short has worked (30y > 5%) — the rare case where a boom-era rates short pays early. The sector-credit short is building but unproven; IG is still tight. History says the big credit move comes when AI revenue fails to match capex, and that arrives after the equity top.

11Seven Lessons

1
The trade is credit, not duration. In five of seven episodes the winning bond short was against the boom's debt — railroads, telecom, shale, LGFV/shadow credit, and (likely) AI data-center structures. Treasury duration shorts lost in 1920s, telecom, Japan (if held), and China. The risk-free curve is a poor expression of a capex boom's risk.
2
Yield peaks lag activity peaks — the "short too late" trap. Japan's JGB peak came ~9 months after the Nikkei top; 1857 and 1873 yield spikes came with/after panics; telecom spreads peaked ~2 years after deterioration began. The boom rarely ends with a top-tick short available; it ends with a reversal that destroys late shorts. Size and stop discipline are everything.
3
The bust's bond rally dwarfs the boom's rise. Japan: +320bp then −800bp. 1870s and 1930s: yields fell for a decade. Even if a short works for a year, staying short past the peak is ruinous. The asymmetry strongly favors being long bonds into the bust — the exact inverse of the boom thesis.
4
Central-bank easing trumps credit risk on the sovereign curve. In Japan (1997–98) and telecom (2001–02), credit risk exploded while government yields collapsed. The sovereign bond always prices policy first. Never short the sovereign purely on credit deterioration — you will be fighting the central bank.
5
The boom's financing structure determines where the short lives. Bond-financed booms (railroads, telecom, shale, AI ABS) concentrate the damage in credit. Bank-financed booms (China) hide it in shadow banking and administered curves. Equity-financed booms (1920s) delay bond damage until defaults arrive years later. Read the funding mix before picking the instrument.
6
The structural tell: no free cash flow. Shale produced −$300bn FCF over a decade; telecom laid 39M fiber miles that were 97% idle; Japan's zaitech earnings exceeded operating profits. When capex is financed by debt that the assets cannot repay on a cash basis, the credit short is fundamentally grounded — but it only pays when the market reprices, which requires a trigger (oil, a fraud, a rate shock, a repo squeeze).
7
For the current AI boom: the playbook says patience, not prophecy. Duration shorts have paid early (30y > 5%) because this boom is unusual — it has direct fiscal/term-premium consequences. But IG is at 20-year tights and issuance is being absorbed greedily, exactly as telecom's was. If history is any guide, the AI credit short that works best will be in HY/ABS/private structures, and its payoff will arrive after the equity and capex peaks — not before. The discipline is to keep powder dry until the credit — not the rates — begins to crack.
